The STAR method helps you turn a real experience into an answer an interviewer can follow. It stands for **Situation, Task, Action, and Result**. The structure matters, but your answer should still sound like you.

## Start with one useful example

Choose a moment that shows the skill behind the question. Paid work is not required. A class project, volunteer shift, sports team, community role, or family responsibility can demonstrate communication, reliability, initiative, or problem-solving.

Write down four short prompts:

- **Situation:** What was happening?
- **Task:** What needed to be achieved?
- **Action:** What did you personally do?
- **Result:** What changed, and what did you learn?

Keep the situation brief. Most of your answer should explain your actions, because that is the clearest evidence of how you work.

## Make the action specific

Weak answers hide behind “we.” Stronger answers explain your contribution without ignoring the team.

Instead of saying, “We fixed the problem,” explain the steps you took: “I checked the order details, confirmed the mistake with my teammate, explained the solution to the customer, and followed up before the end of the shift.”

## Use honest results

A result does not need an impressive number. You can describe a deadline met, a misunderstanding resolved, positive feedback, fewer mistakes, or a lesson you applied later. Never invent a metric.

## Practise points, not a script

Memorise the four ideas rather than every sentence. Say the answer aloud, shorten unnecessary context, and check that the action connects directly to the question. A useful answer is usually clearer than a dramatic one.

> Prepare three flexible examples: one about working with people, one about solving a problem, and one about learning from a mistake.
